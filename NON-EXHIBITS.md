# Non-exhibits

Cases and categories that have been proposed, or that a reader would reasonably expect
to find here, and why each stays out.

This file exists for two reasons. It stops the same suggestions arriving repeatedly, and
it teaches the ambiguity/vagueness line better than a definition can, because the line is
easier to see in the cases that fall on the wrong side of it.

Nothing here is a judgment that the case is uninteresting. Most of these are more famous
than most of the exhibits.

---

## 1. Vagueness, not ambiguity

The largest category by far, and the one that produces the most proposals.

A vague term has one clear grammatical structure and an open-textured predicate. There is
no second parse to write down, only a question about where a boundary falls. No edit
resolves it in advance, because the drafter would first have to decide what the standard
means in a situation nobody has described yet.

**Excluded:** "reasonable efforts" and "best efforts"; "material adverse effect" and
material adverse change; "promptly", "without undue delay", "as soon as practicable";
"substantial", "significant", "commercially reasonable"; "good faith"; "satisfactory
condition".

Compare `frigaliment-what-is-chicken`, which is in the collection. "Chicken" was not vague
at its edges. Two settled trade senses were available, the buyer meant one and the seller
the other, and either could have been specified outright. That is a choice between senses,
not a borderline case of one.

The strongest objection to this whole category is that the line is not always crisp, and
`white-city-v-pr-restaurants` is where the collection feels the pressure. It is filed as a
boundary specimen with the argument against its inclusion stated in full.

## 2. Scrivener's error and mutual mistake

The text is wrong rather than ambiguous. Everyone agrees what the words say and everyone
agrees the words are not what was meant. That is a different problem with a different
remedy, usually rectification or reformation, and it does not belong to interpretation.

**Excluded:** transposed figures, a wrong party name, a cross-reference pointing at a
deleted clause, a schedule that contradicts the operative provision it was drawn from.

The awkward case is `raffles-two-ships-peerless`, taught for over a century as mutual
mistake. It is in the collection, filed as a boundary specimen, with the argument for
excluding it recorded.

---

## 3. Fact-finding

The words are not in contention. What is contested is what happened, what a party
intended, whether a witness is credible, or whether conduct met a standard.

**Excluded:** whether a party actually used the efforts it promised; whether a breach was
material on the facts; whether a signatory had authority; whether a course of dealing
established a usage.

Criminal law is not excluded by this rule. Statutory interpretation in criminal cases
produces some of the sharpest exhibits available, and `pulsifer-and-or-and` is one. What
gets excluded is fact-finding, not a subject matter.

---

## 4. Interpretation without a rival parse

Cases where a court construed a term but no competing reading was ever available, and the
work was done by policy, deference, or purpose rather than by choosing between structures.

**Excluded:** a clause held void as against public policy; a term implied to fill a gap
the text does not address; an ambiguity resolved solely by *contra proferentem* where the
opposing reading was never articulated.

The test is whether Reading A and Reading B can both be written out. If only one can, and
the other is "the drafter should also have covered X", the dispute is about a gap rather
than an ambiguity.

---

## 5. Could not be verified

A case may qualify on every criterion and still be unpublishable if its text cannot be
reached.

An exhibit may ship with `verification.status: needs-primary`, saying what is outstanding
and leaving `resolution.quote` as an explicit placeholder. What it may not do is put
reconstructed wording in `disputed_text`, because that field renders as a quotation and a
status banner does not warn anyone about it.

**Removed under this rule:** *Doe d. Gord v. Needs* (1836) 2 M & W 129, an equivocation
case in a will. No free reproduction could be reached. BAILII and vLex carry the headnote
only, and the Meeson & Welsby volume scanned on archive.org is a different one of the
sixteen.

This leaves a real gap. Equivocation is no longer any exhibit's primary mechanism, and no
testamentary instrument remains. A properly sourced replacement is wanted, and United
States probate is likelier to yield one reachable to a full opinion than a nineteenth
century English report.

---

## 6. Qualifies, but not a fit

A case can satisfy every criterion and still be the wrong thing to put in a collection
meant to be read for pleasure. Where the underlying facts are grave enough that a reader
would be jarred by encountering them as a specimen of punctuation, the case stays out.

This is an editorial judgment rather than an application of the criterion, and it is
recorded as such. It has been applied once.

---

## Proposing something

If you think a rejection here is wrong, argue the criterion rather than the interest of
the case. Show that Reading A and Reading B can both be written out, and that an edit made
in advance, knowing nothing of what later happened, would have foreclosed one of them.

If the case belongs to category 5, the argument to make is a link to the report.
